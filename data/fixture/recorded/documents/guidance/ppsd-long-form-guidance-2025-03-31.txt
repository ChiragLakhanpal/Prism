              Common Terms and Acronyms

The following table sets out common terms (those starting with capital letters) and acronyms that
are used in this Procurement Guidance.

Term or Acronym             Definition
Abnormally Low Bid      A Bid or Proposal in which the price, in combination with other
(ALB) or Abnormally Low elements, appears so low that it raises material concerns with the
Proposal (ALP)          Borrower as to the capability of the Bidder or Proposer to perform
                        the contract for the offered price.
Alternative Procurement     A Bank-approved procurement arrangement that can be used
Arrangement (APA)           instead of applying the Bank’s Procurement Regulations.
Annex                       An Annex to this Procurement Guidance.
Applicant                   A firm or joint venture that submits an Application in response to an
                            invitation for Prequalification or Initial Selection.
Application                 A document submitted by an Applicant in response to an invitation
                            for Prequalification or Initial Selection.
BAFO                        Best and Final Offer.
Bank                        The World Bank, comprising the International Bank for Reconstruction
                            and Development (IBRD) and International Development Association
                            (IDA), whether acting in its own capacity or as administrator of trust
                            funds funded by other donors.
Bid                         An offer, by a firm or joint venture, in response to a Request for
                            Bids, to provide the required Goods, Works, or Non-consulting
                            Services.
Bidder                      A firm or joint venture that submits a Bid for Goods, Works, or Non-
                            consulting Services in response to a Request for Bids.
BOO                         Build, Own, Operate. A project delivery method, usually applied to
                            large-scale infrastructure projects.
BOOT                        Build, Own, Operate, and Transfer. A project delivery method, usually
                            applied to large-scale infrastructure projects.
Borrower                    A Borrower or recipient of Investment Project Financing (IPF) and
                            any other entity involved in the implementation of a Project financed
                            by IPF.
BOT                         Build, Operate, and Transfer. A project delivery method, usually
                            applied to large-scale infrastructure projects.

CDD                         Community-Driven Development.
CIP                         An Incoterm meaning Carriage and Insurance Paid to a named place
                            of destination.

     Term or Acronym               Definition
     Competitive Dialogue          A selection method for Goods, Works or Non-consulting Services
                                   which can be interactive and multistage allowing for dynamic
                                   engagement with Bidders or Proposers Reference the Bank’s
                                   Procurement Regulations, paragraphs 6.39 to 6.41 and Annex XIII.
     Complaint                     A procurement-related complaint which may arise when the Bank’s
                                   Standard Procurement Documents (SPDs) are required to be used.
                                   Reference the Bank’s Procurement Regulations, paragraphs 3.27 to
                                   3.32 and Annex III.
     Consultant                    Private or public entities, joint ventures, or individuals that provide
                                   services of an advisory or professional nature. Where the Consultant is
                                   an individual, they are not engaged by the Borrower as an employee.
     Consulting Services           Arange of services that are of an advisory or professional nature and
                                   are provided by Consultants.
                                   These services typically involve providing expert or strategic advice, such
                                   as management Consultants, policy Consultants, or communications
                                   Consultants. Advisory and Project-related Consulting Services include, for
                                   example, feasibility studies, Project management, engineering services,
                                   finance and accounting services, training, and development.
     Core Procurement              The Bank’s seven Core Procurement Principles comprising, Value for
     Principles                    Money (VfM), economy, integrity, fit for purpose, efficiency, transparency,
                                   and fairness, as described in the Bank’s Policy, Procurement in IPF and
                                   Other Operational Procurement Matters, Section III C.
     CQS                           Consultants Qualifications-based Selection.
     Emergency Situations          Situations when the Borrower, beneficiary, or member country is
                                   deemed by the Bank to be in urgent need of assistance because of a
                                   natural or man-made disaster or conflict.
     Early Market                 A form of meeting (in person or virtual) or communication In Writing
     Engagement                   with representatives of potential bidders and/or industry trade
                                  bodies/ representatives representing whole industries or relevant
                                  segments of a given industry.
     EoI                           Expression of Interest.
     EPC                           Engineering, Procurement, and Construction. A form of contract
                                   used to undertake construction works on large-scale and complex
                                   infrastructure projects.
     EPCM                          Engineering, Procurement, and Construction Management. A form
                                   of contract used to undertake construction works on large-scale and
                                   complex infrastructure projects.
     Evaluation Criteria           Price and non-price criteria, including Rated Criteria when used,
                                   that define how the Borrower assesses which Bidder or Proposer
                                   provides the Most Advantageous Bid or Proposal.
     Framework Agreement           An agreement with one or more firms that establishes the terms and
     (FA)                          conditions that will govern any contract awarded during the term of
                                   the FA.
     FBS                           Fixed-budget Based Selection. A method of evaluating the selection
                                   of Consultants where Proposals are based on a fixed budget.

Term or Acronym          Definition
Fraud and Corruption     Practices that are sanctionable by the Bank, which include,
                         corruption, fraud, collusion, coercion and obstruction. Reference the
                         Bank’s Directive, Guidelines on Preventing and Combating Fraud
                         and Corruption in Projects Financed by IBRD Loans and IDA Credits
                         and Grants and the Bank’s Procurement Regulations, Annex IV.
Goods                    A category of procurement that includes commodities, raw material,
                         machinery, equipment; vehicles, Plant, and related services, such as
                         transportation, insurance, installation, commissioning, training, and
                         initial maintenance.
General Procurement      An advance notice of an international competitive procurement
Notice (GPN)             opportunity in relation to a Procurement Plan. A GPN provides
                         summary details such as, the Borrower, the scope of the procurement,
                         and an indication of when Specific Procurement Notice(s) (SPNs) will
                         be posted. GPNs are published on UNDB and the Bank’s external
                         website.
IBRD                     International Bank for Reconstruction and Development. A World
                         Bank institution.
IDA                      International Development Association. A World Bank institution.
Incoterms                The international commercial terms for Goods published by the
                         International Chamber of Commerce.
Initial Selection (IS)   The Shortlisting process used prior to inviting Proposals when applying
                         a Request for Proposals selection method.
Investment Project       The Bank’s financing of investment Projects through loans, credits,
Financing (IPF)          grants, or guarantees from the Bank’s resources or from trust
                         funds financed by other donors and administered by the Bank,
                         or a combination of these. IFP supports Projects with defined
                         development objectives, activities, and results and aims to promote
                         poverty reduction and sustainable development. IPF is more fully
                         described in the he Bank’s Policy, Investment Project Finance,
                         Section III, paragraph 1.
KPI                      Key Performance Indicator. A quantifiable measure of performance
                         over time related to specific deliverables, objectives or outcomes.
LCS                      Least Cost-based Selection. A method of evaluating the selection of
                         Consultants based on the lowest price.
Legal Agreement          The Legal Agreement that governs the relationship between the
                         Borrower and the Bank in relation to IPF operations. It incorporates the
                         Bank’s Procurement Regulations, and other documents incorporated
                         by reference, such as the Procurement Plan and PPSD. The provisions
                         of the Legal Agreement and documents incorporated by reference are
                         legally binding on the Borrower.
Most Advantageous Bid    The successful Bid or Proposal in accordance with the applicable
or Proposal (MABP)       selection method. Reference the Bank’s Procurement Regulations,
                         paragraphs 5.68 to 5.71 and Annex X, Evaluation Criteria.
NGO                      Non-Governmental Organization.

     Term or Acronym            Definition
     Non-consulting Services    Services that are not Consulting Services. Non-consulting Services
                                are normally bid and contracted on the basis of performance of
                                measurable outputs, and for which performance standards can
                                be clearly identified and consistently applied. Examples include
                                drilling, aerial photography, satellite imagery, mapping, and similar
                                operations.
     PAD                        Project Appraisal Document. A document produced as part of
                                the Bank’s Project cycle which summarizes the expected Project
                                outcomes and other matters.
     PESTLE                     Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental.
                                An analysis tool which supported the assessment of each of these
                                factors.
     PIU                        Project Implementation Unit. The team in the Borrower’s implementation
                                agency responsible for the Project.
     Plant                      The provision of equipped facilities, such as those executed on the
                                basis of design, supply, installation, and commissioning.
     PPP                        Public-Private Partnership. A long-term contract between a private
                                party and a Borrower for providing a public asset or service, in
                                which the private party bears significant risk and management
                                responsibility, and remuneration is linked to performance. Reference
                                the Bank’s Procurement Regulations paragraphs 6.42 to 6.45 and
                                Annex XIV.
     Prequalification (PQ)      The shortlisting process that can be used prior to inviting Request
                                for Bids in the procurement of Goods, Works, or Non-Consulting
                                Services.
     Procurement Documents A generic term used in the Bank’s Procurement Regulations to cover
                           all Procurement Documents issued by the Borrower to potential
                           Applicants, Bidders, or Proposers. Examples include General
                           Procurement Notice (GPN), Specific Procurement Notice (SPN), Request
                           for Expressions of Interest (REoI), Prequalification document, Initial
                           Selection document, Request for Bids (RFB), Request for Proposals
                           (RFP), forms of contracts, and any addenda. When the procurement
                           relates to an IPF financed Project the procurement document is based
                           on a Bank’s Standard Procurement Document (SPD.)
     Procurement Plan (PP)      The Borrower’s Procurement Plan for IPF Projects which is
                                incorporated by reference into the Legal Agreement(s). Reference
                                the Procurement Regulations paragraphs 4.4 and 4.5.
     Procurement Process        The process that starts with the identification of a need and continues
                                through planning, preparation of specifications, or requirements, budget
                                considerations, selection, contract award, contract management. and
                                full contract delivery. When applicable, it ends on the last day of the
                                warranty period.
     Procurement                The World Bank Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers which
     Regulations                apply to procurement in Investment Project Financing (IPF) of Goods,
                                Works, Non-consulting and Consulting Services.
     Project                    A project supported by Bank IPF.

Term or Acronym          Definition
Project Concept Note     A document prepared by the Borrower and the Bank which identifies
                         the Project’s proposed objectives, risks to these objectives’
                         achievement, alternative scenarios, and a likely timetable for the
                         Project preparation process.
Project Development      The specific development objectives that a Project intends to
Objective (PDO)          achieve.
Project Procurement      A Project-level strategy document, prepared by the Borrower, that
Strategy for             describes how procurement in IPF operations supports the development
Development (PPSD)       objectives of the Project and delivers Value for Money (VfM).
Proposal                 An offer, in response to a Request for Proposals, which may or may not
                         include price, by one party to provide Goods, Works, Non-consulting
                         Services, or Consulting Services to another party.
Proposer                 An individual entity or joint venture that submits a Proposal for
                         Goods, Works, and Non-consulting Services in response to a Request
                         for Proposals.
QBS                      Quality-Based Selection. A method that may be used for Consultants
QCBS                     Quality and Cost-Based Selection. A method that may be used for
                         Consultants
Rated Criteria           Non-price factors that are taken into account to measure quality
                         aspects when evaluating Bids or Proposals. Examples include,
                         quality, sustainability, value for money opportunities, innovation,
                         other technical aspects, and risk mitigation strategies. Reference the
                         Bank’s Procurement Regulations, Paragraph 5.50.
REoI                     Request for Expressions of Interest.
RFB                      Request for Bids.
RFP                      Request for Proposals.
RFQ                      Request for Quotations.
Shortlist                The process used prior to issuing a Request for Proposals (RFP) in
                         the procurement of Consulting Services.
Specific Procurement     A notice that advertises a business opportunity. Examples include,
Notice (SPN)             Request for Bids, Request for Proposals. For international competitive
                         procurements SPNs are published on UNDB Online and the Bank’s
                         external website.
Standard Procurement     Procurement documents issued by the Bank to be used by Borrowers
Documents (SPDs)         for IPF Projects. These include the General Procurement Notice (GPN),
                         Standard Procurement Notice (SPN), Request for Expressions of Interest
                         (REoI), Prequalification document, Initial Selection document, Request
                         for Bids (RFB), Request for Proposals (RFP), and forms of contract.
State-Owned Enterprise   A legal entity that is created by a government in order to participate
(SOE)                    in commercial activities on the government’s behalf. It can be either
                         wholly or partially owned by a government.
Subcriteria              A subsection of the Evaluation Criteria, including a subcriteria to a
                         Rated Criteria, to which a Bidder is required to respond.

     Term or Acronym             Definition
     Supply Positioning          The Supply Positioning model is a strategic procurement tool that
                                 helps classify and prioritize an organization’s procurement activities
                                 based on the strategic importance and risk associated with each item.
     SWOT                        Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. An analysis
                                 tool that supports capability assessment, market analysis and
                                 procurement risk assessment.
     UN Agency/Agencies          Broadly refers to the United Nations departments and specialized
                                 agencies, as well as their regional offices, funds, and programs.
     Value for Money (VfM)       One of the Bank’s Core Procurement Principles meaning the
                                 effective, efficient, and economic use of resources, which requires
                                 the evaluation of relevant costs and benefits, along with an
                                 assessment of risks, and of non-price attributes and, or lifecycle
                                 costs, as appropriate. The lowest priced Bid or Proposal alone may
                                 not necessarily represent Value for Money.
     Works                       A category of procurement that refers to construction, repair,
                                 rehabilitation, demolition, restoration, and maintenance of civil
                                 work structures, as well as related services, such as transportation,
                                 insurance, installation, commissioning, and training. It also includes
                                 water and sanitation, transportation and energy-related infrastructure.

     Where there is an inconsistency between a common term or acronym and a Defined Term
     in the Bank’s Procurement Policy, Directive, Procedure or Procurement Regulations forming
     the mandatory Procurement Framework, the Defined Term shall take precedence. For more
     information on the Bank’s Procurement Framework visit the Bank’s webpage Procurement
     Framework for IPF Projects.

              Introduction



Project Procurement Strategy for Development
A Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD) is the Bank’s Project-level procurement
strategy document. The preparation of the PPSD by Borrowers, with the support of Bank staff,
is the primary procurement planning instrument for Projects.

Developing the PPSD is part of the preparation process for an Investment Project Financing
(IPF) plan. The PPSD describes how procurement in the IPF operations will support the Project’s
development objectives and deliver Value for Money (VfM). Based on the PPSD, Borrowers are
able to develop the Procurement Plan (PP). The PP documents the agreed-upon Procurement
Arrangements for the Project.

There are two PPSD templates, namely:

   1.	 Short-Form PPSD

   2.	 Long-Form PPSD



This Guidance
This Guidance details a structured approach to preparing a PPSD Long Form. It describes various
tools and methodologies that support the development of a comprehensive procurement strategy.

Each PPSD should be fit for purpose. This means that the level of detail should be proportionate
to the Project-specific risks, contract value, context, nature, and complexity. The application of
the tools and methodologies described in this Guidance and the level of detailed analysis should
therefore be commensurate with these factors. There is no expectation that all the tools and
methodologies will be used—only those that are most appropriate, and to a level of detail that is
consistent with the nature of the Project.

To assist the user, this Guidance is structured in the same sequence as the information required
in the PPSD template. However, as the Borrower completes the PPSD sequentially, there may be
a need to revisit earlier sections as a result of subsequent information obtained or research and
analysis undertaken.

Strategy and Planning
The Project Procurement for Development Strategy (PPSD) and the Procurement Plan (PP)
are documents that the Borrower is required to produce as part of the preparation process for
Investment Project Financing (IPF) plans. PPSDs should conform to the appropriate PPSD template
(Long Form or Short Form).



Project Cycle
The preparation of the PPSD should begin as early as possible in the Project Cycle. This is normally
during the Project Concept stage, once the major contracts in the Project have been identified.
Where circumstances allow, the Borrower should begin the preparation of the PPSD after the
project feasibility stage, in which decisions on the technical solution and associated contract types
start to be made.



Process Overview
The World Bank’s Procurement Regulations describe the requirements in paragraphs 4.1 to 4.3.
In summary they are:


Preparation
The Borrower prepares the PPSD and PP during the Project preparation stage for each IPF Project.
The level of detail in the PPSD should be proportionate to the risk, value, context, nature, and
complexity of the Project. The information available to support the development of the PPSD will be
different for each Project, and there may be information gaps that need to be addressed through
additional research or market engagement.

   1.	 One single, Long-Form PPSD is prepared for all contracts that are required for a Project.
       The PPSD addresses how procurement activities will support the development objectives
       of the Project and deliver the best Value for Money (VfM) under a risk-based approach.
       It also outlines the Borrower’s procurement objectives, proposed evaluation approach and
       methodology, and the various stages of evaluation. Where Rated Criteria are to be used,

Bank Oversight
PPSDs are subjected to Bank oversight at various stages of the Bank’s Project cycle.

   1.	 No formal clearance of the PPSD is required. The Bank reviews the PPSD and agrees to the
       PP before the completion of loan negotiations.

   2.	 A summary of the PPSD forms part of the Bank’s Project Appraisal Document (PAD). The
       Bank will consider the PPSD summary as part of the overall PAD package and determine
       whether the whole package can be cleared.

   3.	 The Bank reviews the PPSD and PP as part of the Project appraisal and reaches agreement with
       the Borrower on the PP by no later than completion of loan negotiations. The PP, approved by the
       Bank during loan negotiations, is incorporated by reference in the Legal Agreement, making it
       legally binding on the Borrower.

   4.	 Once it has provided its no-objection, the Bank arranges for the publication of the PP and all
       of its updates on its external website.

The Borrower may prepare a high-level, simplified PPSD in situations of urgent need of assistance

The PPSD is the cornerstone for ensuring that the procurement approach is properly planned and

The PPSD is structured to ensure that consideration is given to all the appropriate procurement tools,
techniques, and methods at this planning stage. It is also structured to provide the earliest opportunity

    1. Design
    The Design stage is documented in the PPSD and will result in a set of proposed fit-for-purpose
    procurement approaches for the contracts within the Project. These approaches will be justified
    by appropriate and proportionate levels of research and analysis. A fit-for-purpose procurement
    approach is the optimum tailored procurement approach for delivering the Project Development
    Objectives (PDOs), taking into consideration the individual circumstances and business and
    operating environments of the particular procurements.


    2. Demonstrate
    The Demonstrate stage addresses the evaluation of Bids and Proposals. It is a critical procurement
    stage and must be planned carefully to ensure that the evaluation method considers a range
    of criteria that add value, reduce risk, and evaluate costs over the whole of life, as appropriate.
    Getting the evaluation method right ensures that suppliers are given the best opportunity to
    demonstrate their value proposition throughout the selection process.

    At this stage, the Borrower determines the most appropriate type of Evaluation Criteria to be
    applied. This includes consideration of the use of Rated Criteria to assess non-price, technical, or

quality attributes. International procurements advertised on or after March 1, 2025, using a Bank
SPD, must use Rated Criteria and apply Rated Criteria weightings as detailed in the Matrix of Rated
Criteria weightings.4 Rated Criteria and any subcriteria need to be described, prioritized, and
weighted by the Borrower. The Borrower then determines the final weightings to be applied to the

balance of quality and price. The overall technical versus financial cost weighting— for example a
technical evaluation of 80 percent compared to a financial cost of 20 percent—denotes the
determined optimum balance of quality factors and cost in a procurement and drives the
assessment of best VfM. The percentage split in the overall weighting must follow the Matrix of

     Operational Context
     This section comprises four aspects: Governance, Economic, Sustainability, and Technological. The
     assessment should provide analysis of each aspect and describe the considerations that impact the
     operational context and that may influence the procurement approach, the motivation of Bidders to
     participate, as well as the success of any subsequent contracts. Most of this information is available
     through research and analysis undertaken by the Bank and presented in the Project preparation
     documents for the Project and in other similar Projects.

       the market or Bidders; the overall legal framework; and disaster or Emergency

       be taken into account when considering challenges faced by Borrowers to integrate

       that need to be in place for sustainable procurement to be successful, including the legal

     Market Research and Analysis
     The objective of market research and analysis is to develop an appropriate understanding of the market
     sectors that have been identified as having the potential to Bid for the Goods, services, or Works
     required for a contract within a Project. This is to ensure that the eventual procurement approach
     maximizes the likelihood of the right Bidders participating, and the right supplier being selected, thereby
     minimizing failed Procurement Processes due to a lack of Bids or the wrong type of Bid.

     This section will explain how a market works and how this impacts the Borrower’s approach to the
     market. It will look at the market from both the perspective of the Borrower and suppliers operating
     in that space, to determine what will motivate the right suppliers to participate in the procurement.

     This information should then be analyzed to design the procurement approach to ensure that the
     right markets and suppliers are targeted, so that the procurement approach maximizes market
                                                                                            PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS   17




interest—noting that high-value and profile procurement is not always sufficient motivation for the
right Bidders to participate.

Most Projects will have multiple individual procurement contracts, many of which will be low value
and low risk. A Supply Positioning model should be used to assess how much analysis each of the
contracts in a Project will require, ensuring that a proportionate approach is taken on the overall
analysis of all contracts.

Outcome
On completion of the Supply Positioning assessment, the Borrower should use their judgment to make
sure that the contract placement on the model is realistic. The use of Supply Positioning at this stage of
the PPSD preparation is designed to help Borrowers identify where effort needs to be focused.

Where data or information is not available, the Borrower should state their “assumptions” and use
their best judgment. These assumptions and judgments should be reviewed as the PPSD is being
prepared, and if necessary, the Borrower should revisit the Supply Positioning assessment, if there
are any material changes to the assumptions or judgments made.

Markets
The definition of a market is broad. It can be where:

   1.	 Buyers and sellers come together

   2.	 Providers of Goods, Works, and Services operate

Markets can be complex, and they are often segmented based upon factors such as scale,
specializations, or geography; for example, transport subsegments can include heavy engineering,
rural road infrastructure, highways, and bridges.

Some suppliers can be generalists, covering a whole segment, while others may be specialists,
operating in a single subsegment.

Therefore, a market analysis needs to consider the supply chain and identify the markets and
suppliers that exist at different points in the supply chain. For example, in a construction market,
this could be quarries, converters, manufacturers, installers, Consultants, and prime contractors.

For more information, see:

Procurement Guidance: Supply Chain Management


Why Perform Market Analysis?
The benefits of market analysis include the following:

   1.	 It will ensure that contract lots are designed to align with the target market.

   2.	 Supplier awareness, engagement, and opinion will lead to increased confidence in the
       procurement approach being followed and encourage a competitive Bid.
20       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




        3.	 	 Insight into market possibilities and potential innovative solutions will be gained.

        4.	 A better understanding of risk at an earlier stage of the Project Cycle will ensure
            that the selected procurement approach is designed to manage or mitigate
             identified risks.

        5.	 There will be a better understanding of where a Project’s requirements fit into the
            marketplace, the marketplace’s capability of meeting those requirements, and how the
            requirements could either be shaped to fit the marketplace, or how the latter must be
            shaped to meet the requirements of the former.



     Market Analysis Scope
     Market analysis is undertaken to gain a common understanding of the current supply market in
     order to be able to clearly:

        	1.	Identify the type of market

        	2.	 Describe the nature of competition and how it really works

        	3.	 Establish supply market capability

        	4.	 Estimate the total and available supply market capacity

        	5.	 Know the factors influencing the market, and how they may affect Bidder participation

        	6.	 Understand how factors influencing the market may drive change and the impact of
             this change

        	7.	Know who the key suppliers are and their plans for the future

        	8.	 Identify costs associated with the supply of Goods, Works, and Consulting or Non-consulting
             Services

        	9.	 Identify pricing methods used by the suppliers in this market

        	10.	 Establish pricing trends in terms of actual prices and pricing methods

        	11.	 Understand current best-practice pricing methods and the availability of cost and price
              benchmarks

        	12.	 Identify risk associated with a market and describe how the risk should be managed

        	13.	 Assess the Borrower’s previous experience achieving VfM when operating in
              similar markets

        	14.	 Describe how other customers in the market achieve VfM, and get the right result
                                                                          PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS          21




   	15.	 Determine whether the Borrower’s contract is likely to have any positive or negative impact
         on the market. Will the right suppliers bid? Will those suppliers bid in the right way to get the
         right result?

     Market Engagement
     Market engagement is a way of discussing the Borrower’s requirements with a marketplace and the
     suppliers it contains. It is particularly useful if there is a need to create a market or increase supplier
                                                                    PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS       25




participation due to a lack of market interest, especially where there is an incumbent supplier,
or the requirement is novel.

In undertaking market engagement, Borrowers should ensure the core principles of fairness
and transparency are upheld by treating all potential suppliers equally. It is particularly important
that the Borrower manages the market engagement in a manner to avoid perceived or actual
conflict of interest situations in the Procurement Process, including but not restricted to, the
market engagement itself, the final requirements or specifications, and the selected Procurement
Arrangements.

Effective market engagement can help establish the marketplace’s ability to meet the Borrower’s
requirements, provide ideas on alternative approaches for meeting these specifications, highlight
potential risks early in the Project Cycle, and provide an insight into likely marketplace interest.
Simply, it can also motivate the right suppliers to bid. This information should then be used
to develop a procurement approach that manages risk in the right way for everyone, is more
attractive overall to the marketplace, generates greater competition, and ultimately maximizes
the opportunities to meet the Borrower’s PDOs.

There are many different ways in which market engagement can be undertaken. These include
concept viability exercises, supplier questionnaires, market sounding exercises, supplier
conferences, trade events, paid-for market research, and publication of outline procurement
strategies for consultation.

Borrowers should consider the following when undertaking any market engagement:

   ◾	 Engage early and widely with the marketplace and suppliers to give them an opportunity to
      shape the requirement.

   ◾	 Work with all suppliers on an equal basis to ensure openness of access to staff and information.

   ◾	 Establish good communication channels and keep potential Bidders informed.

   ◾	 Maintain genuine competition throughout the process; do not leave Bidders in the
      competition if you do not believe they are capable of winning.

   ◾	 Be transparent about the procedures and top-level criteria for evaluation of Bids.

   ◾	 Maintain the commercial confidentiality of information received from suppliers.

   ◾	 Be willing to consider commissioning pilots, paid studies, and Proof of Concept exercises,
      as well as Design and Build exercises.

   ◾	 Be open to novel approaches.

   ◾	 Focus on the desired outcomes.

   ◾	 Be willing to reconsider the requirement and scope, and the packaging of the requirement
      for procurement.

        ◾	 Ensure the commitment and support of senior management in dealing with the marketplace.

     assess how important the Borrower is to that supplier as a customer.

PPSD Section 3: Procurement Risk Analysis
The Importance of Risk Assessment
Procurement risk analysis is the process of identifying and minimizing the likelihood of a risk
occurring, as well as minimizing the impact to the Borrower’s PDOs, business, and stakeholders
if the risk does occur. It also looks at identifying and maximizing the likelihood of an opportunity
occurring, and thereby maximizing the impact or benefit to the Borrower’s PDOs, business, and
stakeholders if the opportunity materializes. The procurement risk analysis sets out the plan for
tackling risks and opportunities.

All of the risks identified from section 4 of this Guidance are summarized and evaluated in a
Procurement Risk Management Plan, as seen in table VI. This plan defines the risks, assesses
the impact, describes the mitigation action, and allocates the risk to an owner.

Risk management supports the recognition, evaluation, and prioritization of potential failures and
their effects. It enables project delivery by identifying and tracking actions, which eliminates or
mitigates the chance of the potential failure occurring. Its intent is to maximize positive impact
and minimize adverse impact to the procurement approach and procurement objectives.

     Risk Management Plan
     Table VI should be used to create the Risk Management Plan by summarizing and recording the
     overall risk management analysis.

     Procurement risk management is primarily concerned with managing impacts on the contract
     schedule, cost, and performance, including the delivery of the stated requirements. Through a
     structured approach to risk management, Borrowers should identify how opportunities and risks
     will be managed at different stages of the Procurement Process. The following Procurement
     Process stages should be used as a starting point to complete table VI.

        1.	 Market engagement — Make certain that the Borrower understands how the market
            functions and demonstrate to suppliers the benefits of working with the Borrower.

        2.	 Prequalification — Ensure that only suppliers capable of delivering the requirements are
            invited to bid.

        3.	 Specification — Verify that the Borrower clearly states the detailed specification or
            performance requirements and provides the supplier with freedom to use its expertise
            to be innovative in its delivery.

        4.	 Procurement Arrangements — Ascertain that Bids are competitive and ultimately deliver
            demonstrable VfM. Examples include the contract type, conditions of contract, and pricing
            mechanisms.

        5.	 Evaluation — Establish that the most appropriate supplier is selected, and all risks are
            managed and understood.

        6.	 Contract Management — Check to ensure that suppliers fulfill their contractual obligations
            and that any issues are dealt with efficiently and effectively in a fair and transparent manner.


     Procurement Risk Analysis
     There are numerous approaches to performing a risk analysis. The PPSD uses a technique that
     looks at identifying the risk (the problem), the likelihood of the risk (event) occurring, the criticality
     of the risk if it occurs, and the impact of the problem (risk). The combination of these factors allows
     a determination of the risk rating.

     To make the identification and analysis of procurement risk manageable, the Borrower should
     structure the assessment around the following eight key areas:

        1.	 Market complexity and competitiveness

        2.	 Delivery and supply security

        3.	 Suppliers and supplier relationships

        4.	 Borrower experience, capacity, and capability
                                                                       PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS        31




   5.	 Cost trends

   6.	 Technical innovation—the degree and rate of change

   7.	 Sustainability—environmental, economic, social

   8.	 Business and operating environment

Quantifying risk and allocating a rating is subjective, but the use of the following criteria, in
conjunction with information from the Strategic Analysis in section 4 of this Guidance, should help
the Borrower make the analysis more objective. Factors include:

   ◾	 Previous experience and judgment of the Borrower

   ◾	 Understanding key decision drivers of both suppliers and the Borrower

   ◾	 Identification of key delivery dependencies

   ◾	 Analysis of any assumptions made

   ◾	 Root cause analysis

Once the risks have been identified, they need to be assessed, rated, and ranked by priority. Each
risk, considering likelihood and impact, can then be rated in terms of criticality using the following
ratings. This will provide a classification of the criticality of the risk in terms of overall risk to the
procurement objectives.

   1 = Very Low (VL)

   2 = Low (L)

   3 = Medium (M)

   4 = High (H)

   5 = Very High (VH)

The Borrower should plot each risk on a risk criticality matrix based upon its rating (figure VII). The
matrix provides the Borrower with an indication of what the priority is for each risk, by segmenting
the risk classification into four main groupings.

It may not be possible to address all risks through the procurement approach and the risk
management plan, for reasons such as resource shortages or lack of practical mitigation measures.
The risk matrix will indicate if the residual risk is too high, which would be indicated by a number of
red and yellow ratings remaining unresolved.

Importance of Stakeholder Identification and Management
In this section of the PPSD, the key stakeholders are identified with an interest in or impact on the
Project so that their respective view can be appropriately taken into account in the design of the
procurement approach. The output of this section is a Stakeholder Management Plan (table VII) that
outlines how each stakeholder will be engaged throughout the Procurement Process to ensure that
their expectations and objectives are appropriately met and managed.

     A stakeholder analysis should be performed early in development of the PPSD. Identifying
     and managing stakeholder relationships is a critical part of managing a Project. Stakeholders
     are a good source of information, can influence the ease with which a Project is delivered,
     and some stakeholders will be the recipient of the requirements delivered. Involving these
     individuals in the design of the procurement approach and management of the contract(s)
     will increase ownership.

     Stakeholder analysis is focused on each individual stakeholder’s needs from a Project perspective,
     as well as what their ongoing involvement should be after the contract award. This analysis can be
     undertaken in the following three stages:

        1.	 Identify the stakeholders through stakeholder mapping.

        2.	 Establish their interest or requirements from the Project, defined as Stakeholder Objectives.

        3.	 Specify their ongoing involvement through stakeholder categorization by completing a
            Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed (RACI) analysis.



     Stakeholder Identification
     A stakeholder is someone who is affected by or can affect the outcome of an activity within the
     Project. This includes:

        ◾	 People who can influence the Project

        ◾	 People who want a say in which procurement approach is selected

        ◾	 People who can make a Project fail or succeed through participation or opposition

        ◾	 Those who can contribute financial and technical resources

        ◾	 Those whose attitude has to change for the Project to succeed

     Typically, stakeholders are:

        ◾	 Governments: Federal and State

        ◾	 Beneficiaries

        ◾	 Individuals who are impacted by the Project

        ◾	 Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs), Civil Society, and special interest groups

        ◾	 Other Borrowers in cross-jurisdictional procurements

        ◾	 Bank functions and Task Team members

     Stakeholder mapping is undertaken by brainstorming who has an interest in the Project, from
     conception all the way through to Project delivery.
                                                                    PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS      35




Identifying Each Stakeholder’s Objectives
After the stakeholders have been identified, the next step is to define their objectives. In order to
keep stakeholders’ objectives focused on the procurement approach, stakeholder engagement
needs to be framed around the following aspects:

   1.	 Identifying the issues and challenges that the Borrower has with the procurement or with the
       supply market

   2.	 Identifying the needs and requirements by establishing what the drivers are by considering:

      a.	 What do the beneficiaries/users need from this Project?

      b.	 What does the Borrower require of this supply market and suppliers?

   3.	 Identifying what the Bank expects from the Project

This approach is aimed at capturing both current and future objectives and considering them
as must-haves or wants, which are essential requirements, a stakeholder would not accept a
procurement approach without the possibility of them being delivered. These needs therefore must
be specific and easy to understand so that they can be defined and summarized into a Stakeholder’s
Objective for the Procurement.

Borrowers should confirm this information by talking directly with stakeholders whenever possible.
However, this is not always possible, so for those stakeholders who cannot be contacted directly,
the best approach is for the project team to theorize what that stakeholder’s views may be.

Tool: RACI Analysis
Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed (RACI) is a management tool used to
categorize a stakeholder’s interest and influence over the Stakeholder’s Objectives.
For each objective, a stakeholder’s interest can be categorized as:

   R 5 Responsible	      A person who performs an activity or a task successfully

   A 5 Accountable	A person who is ultimately accountable for the quality and overall success
                    of delivery of the project

   C 5 Consulted	A person who provides feedback and contributes to an activity or task,
                  where the input adds value and is essential for successful implementation

   I 5 Informed	
                A person who needs to be notified of decisions, results, or action taken but
                does not need to be involved in the decision-making or delivery

Asking the following questions should help Borrowers define stakeholder interest and influence
and allow a stakeholder to be categorized under a RACI.

   1.	 What are their needs, influencing factors, and likes?

   2.	 Why might they object to or oppose something?

   3.	 How will they be affected personally by the outcome of the Project?
36       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




        4.	 What is their responsibility?

        5.	 What is their power and level of influence?

            a.	 What is their interest?

            b.	 How do they expect to be involved and why?

        6.	 How predictable are they?

     The list of stakeholders and their objectives may be long and, in some cases, may conflict with
     each other. Further stakeholder engagement may be required to clarify conflicting objectives,
     as well as to prioritize the key objectives that the procurement approach must deliver.

PPSD Section 5: Procurement Objectives
The Importance of Defining Procurement Objectives
This section of the PPSD looks at how to develop procurement objectives that align with the
PDOs. The procurement objectives will be used to test alternative procurement approaches to
ensure that they deliver the Project requirements.

Defining the right procurement objectives is critical. The Borrower should use these objectives as
the basis for:

   1.	 Refining the specification of requirements and supplier incentives

   2.	 Establishing the allocation of risk between the Borrower and the supplier

   3.	 Developing the Evaluation Criteria used to select the supplier who will deliver the contract

   4.	 Documenting the Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) that will be used to monitor contract
       delivery to ensure that the contract continues to meet the Project requirements.

Figure X illustrates the relationship between each of these factors.


Drafting Procurement Objectives
The Borrower should develop key procurement objectives using information already gathered in
sections III and VI of this Guidance, respectively, on the PDOs and Stakeholder Analysis (figure IX).
The key procurement objectives should be detailed enough that, if achieved, will support the delivery
of the Project’s Development Objectives and achieve VfM (table IX).


Tool: RAQSCI Model
RAQSCI stands for Regulation, Assurance of Supply, Quality, Service, Cost, and Innovation. The
RAQSCI model is a tool that the Borrower should use to structure and organize the procurement
objectives. It is also an effective way to ensure that the procurement objectives are comprehensive.
Failure to identify all of the procurement objectives at this stage could result in an ineffective
procurement approach being designed and selected. Figure XI describes the various components
of a RAQSCI Model.

Summarizing Options
This section of the PPSD summarizes the options for the procurement approach for each of the
contracts in the Project. The supporting evidence or justification for each procurement approach
should be drawn from the previous sections of the PPSD.

procurement approach.

   1.	 Procurement approach stating how the Borrower is going to approach the market, select

       methods that combine to produce a bidding document

   3.	 Procurement Plan that summarizes how each contract within the Project will be procured.

All of the analysis and research undertaken as part of the Long-Form PPSD needs to be brought
together to design the different procurement approaches that are available to deliver the procurement
objectives. It is likely that there will be a number of different procurement approaches available.
This section is concerned with generating alternative procurement approaches that could deliver the
procurement objectives and then determine which one is most likely to succeed.

The selection of the recommended procurement approach should only take place once all realistic
options have been identified, described, and appraised. At a minimum, the options analysis should
describe each procurement approach option in sufficient detail to enable evaluation of the options
in terms of their ability to satisfy the procurement objectives.

Types of Competition
Knowledge of the type of competition that exists is a prerequisite to being able to develop the
right procurement approach. For example, in a monopolistic or oligopolistic market, in which one
or few supplier(s) hold all the power, designing a procurement approach that is characterized by
competitive bidding, with significant risk transfer to the supplier and with supplier selection based
on lowest-evaluated cost, is not likely to be successful. In these circumstances, a negotiated
approach based on open-book pricing, potentially linked to a longer-term contract, is more likely
to be effective. Therefore, the type of market will have a direct influence on the procurement and
will help the Borrower determine:

   ◾	 The type of competitive bidding, as determined by the level of completion and the
      availability of substitutes or alternatives

   ◾	 The most effective pricing, or costing mechanism, as determined by the market pricing approach

   ◾	 The level of risk transfer the market will realistically bear, as determined by the relative
      strength and power of the buyer and the supplier’s motivation to participate.

for a more detailed assessment of competition and the factors that determine the type and level
of competition, if further evaluation of the marketplace is required to support the design of the
procurement approach

Requirements
While the PPSD is not directly concerned with the drafting of Project and contract requirements,

   ◾	 The selection of procurement methods, such as bidding approach and appropriate Standard

   ◾	 The transfer of risk between the Borrower and the supplier

   ◾	 The level of supplier innovation

   ◾	 The Bid or Proposal evaluation method and complexity

   ◾	 The likelihood of delivering the PDOs

   ◾	 The level of supplier flexibility in design and delivery

   ◾	 The level of Borrower control over design and delivery

     Conformance Specification
     Conformance specifications are used when a thorough understanding of the requirements already
     exists, and there is little to no desire for the supplier to innovate. In these circumstances the Borrower
     normally has a comprehensive understanding of the requirements and is able to describe them
     in detail. This includes its technical, design, and functional requirements, as well as being able to
     describe exactly how the supplier must perform and deliver the requirements.

     Conformance specifications work best for simple purchases of Goods, services, and Works,
     in which there is a focus on defining specific quantities and specifications for the requirements and
     unit price costing, and specifics around the time, place, and manner for delivery and acceptance.

     The main risk of a conformance specification is if the specification is incorrect, such as if the design
     does not function. Then, all the risk lies with the Borrower because they have specified exactly
     what they want.


     Performance Specification
     Performance specifications are used when the understanding of what is required in terms of
     outcomes can be described, the Borrower is uncertain of the best process or method to deliver the
     requirements, or suppliers are known to have the capability to design fit-for-purpose solutions.

     Performance-based specifications focus on outcomes or results rather than detailing the process of
     production, construction, and delivery. They are particularly effective at allowing suppliers to bring
     their own expertise, creativity, innovation, and resources to the bidding process without restricting
     them to predetermined methods or detailed processes. This allows suppliers to reduce cost and
     passes the risk of both cost and performance, in terms of delivering a solution that meets the
     Borrower’s requirements, to the supplier.

     As a general rule, performance-based specifications focus on achieving results, whereas
     conformance specifications focus on meeting specified design and resource requirements.

     Performance-based specifications should be drafted in such a way that suppliers are allowed
     maximum flexibility when meeting the requirements, whereas conformance specifications exclude
     flexibility, unless alternative Bids are permitted.

     Additional context on specification types and writing specifications can be found in Annex C.


     Aggregated Procurement
     Aggregated procurement involves combining discrete elements of procurement requirements,
     lots, or processes into a single package to achieve cost savings, improved market attractiveness,
     better supplier terms, and streamlined processes. The project PPSD should assess the potential
     for the use of aggregation within a project, setting out where opportunities are and how these will
     be assessed in more detail as procurements progress. This could involve combining lots or
     permitting bidders to bid for multiple lots in a procurement.

Additional Sustainable Procurement Requirements (Non-mandatory)
Sustainable procurement derives from the concept of sustainable development, which has been
defined as “Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of
future generations to meet their own needs.” Sustainable development includes three pillars for
consideration: environmental, social, and economic.The inclusion of additional sustainable
procurement requirements in a procurement approach, beyond those required by the Bank, is
discretionary, so the Borrower can determine the extent to which they implement further
sustainable procurement practice—provided these practices are applied in ways that are
consistent with the Bank’s Core Procurement Principles. A proportionate approach should be
followed when setting requirements for the additional sustainable procurement management
measures.

Generic sustainable procurement requirements can be applied in any contract. However, consideration
of additional specific, sustainable procurement requirements is most effective when the unique risks,
identified in the procurement risk analysis and associated with each Project, are individually addressed.

The intention to adopt additional sustainable procurement requirements in the Procurement
Process should be identified at the planning stage of the PPSD.

Contract Strategy
A Contract Strategy determines the contract type, the price and cost model, and the desired
ongoing supplier relationship to achieve the right result over the term of the contract.


Contract Type
The contract type is the selection of the appropriate contract form, in addition to the terms and
conditions. The type of specification, the procurement risk assessment, and the market analysis
should inform the selection of contract type. It should also consider the market’s capability, the
Borrower’s capacity, and the operational environment in which delivery will be taking place.

The selection of contract type is Project-specific, so consideration also needs to be given to the
procurement objectives. The contract type for Goods and Services is normally on the basis of supply
or supply and ongoing support, while infrastructure Projects have a larger range of options, including
   ◾ Traditional: Design and Build separated

   ◾ Design and Build

        ◾	 Design, Build, Operate, Maintain

        ◾	 Design and Build: Turnkey or Prime Contractor

        ◾	 EPC and EPCM

        ◾	 Design, Build, and Operate

        ◾	 Build, Operate, and Transfer


     Pricing and Costing Mechanism
     The choice of the price and costing mechanism should be made at the time the contract type and
     SPD/bidding document are selected. Each pricing and costing mechanism will determine the risk
     allocation between the Borrower and supplier. Therefore, the cost and price selection should be
     informed by the contract strategy, type of specification, the procurement risk assessment, and
     strategic analysis from section 4 of this Guidance.

     There are five main pricing and costing models, and their use is determined by the SPD/bidding
     document selected:

        1.	 Lump Sum

        2.	 Performance-Based Contracts

        3.	 Schedule of Rates/Admeasurement: Contract based on unit prices

        4.	 Time and Materials: Time-based contracts

        5.	 Cost Plus: Reimbursable cost contracts

     The following is a more detailed description of each of the five costing models.

     Lump Sum Contracts
     In a lump sum contract, the supplier agrees to perform the scope of services for a fixed contract
     amount. Payment percentages or amounts may be linked to the completion of contractual
     milestones or determined as a percentage of the value of the work to be performed.

     Lump sum contracts may be appropriate when:

        ◾	 The scope of the contract can be clearly and accurately specified and can be linked to
           apparent milestones and payments at the time of selection—for example, simple civil Works
           or Consulting Services with clearly identifiable deliverables—and/or

        ◾	 The supplier is responsible for delivering the completed Works—for example, an industrial
           Plant or prebuilt information technology solutions, such as in turnkey contracts—and paid on
           a lump sum basis per contractual milestones.
                                                                    PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS         51




Performance-Based Contracts
Performance Based Contracts are contractual relationships in which payments are made for
measured outputs, or performance targets, instead of inputs. The outputs aim at satisfying
functional needs in terms of quality, quantity, and reliability.

Performance-based contracts may be appropriate for:

   ◾	 The rehabilitation of roads and operation and maintenance of the roads by a contractor for
      specified periods

   ◾	 The provision of Non-consulting Services to be paid on the basis of outputs

   ◾	 The operation of a facility to be paid based on functional performance

Schedule of Rates /Admeasurement
Admeasurement contracts are based on estimated quantities of items and contractually agreed
unit prices for each of these items. Payment is calculated based on the actual quantities of items
used or delivered and applying the agreed-upon contractual unit rate to determine the price to
be paid.

This type of contract is appropriate for Works, when the nature of the work is well defined but the
quantities cannot be determined with reasonable accuracy in advance of construction, such as
roads or dams.

For Goods and Non-consulting Services, it is appropriate when the required quantities are known,
and unit prices are sought from Bidders.

Time and Materials
Time-based contracts are where the payment is made based on agreed rates and time spent, plus
reasonable incurred reimbursable expenses.

These types of contractual arrangements may be used for:

   ◾	 Emergency Situations, repairs, and maintenance Works

   ◾	 Consulting Services, when it is difficult to define or fix the scope and duration of the services,
      such as complex studies, supervision of construction, and advisory services.

This type of contract is not appropriate for Goods and industrial Plants.

Cost Plus
Cost Plus contracts are contracts that require reimbursable payments for all actual costs plus an
agreed fee to cover overhead and profit.

These types of contracts may be appropriate for exceptional fit-for-purpose circumstances, such as
emergency repairs and maintenance work. To minimize risk to the Borrower, suppliers under these

     contracts must make all their records and accounts available for inspection by the Borrower or by
     some agreed-upon neutral third party. The contract should also include appropriate incentives to
     limit costs.

Selection of the Cost and Price Mechanism
When selecting the appropriate pricing and costing mechanism, the Borrower should consider:

    ◾	 Specification Type: The method of specifying the requirements can be conformance-
       based or performance-based. The specification may take into account how well the
       requirements can be scoped at the contract outset and how quickly the work needs
       to start.

    ◾	 Contract Type: Certain types of contracts look to pass risk to the supplier—such as Design,
       Build, Operate, and Maintain—therefore, the price mechanism needs to be one that is
       consistent with that approach.

    ◾	 Required Allocation of Risk: Risk is allocated to the party best able to manage the risk.

    ◾	 Operational Environment: Includes events that are difficult to predict and known issues,
       such as high inflation, delivery, and insurance—covered by the use of Incoterms, known as
       international commercial terms

    ◾	 Capacity of the Borrower: Familiarity with the pricing mechanism and availability of
       resources to manage the contract

    ◾	 Type of Market: What will motivate suppliers to bid and deliver

Price Adjustments
World Bank Standard Procurement Documents (SPDs) offer the option for either fixed prices or
adjustable price contracts, in which adjustments are made to reflect any changes in major cost
activities of the contract, such as labor and materials. For procurements, where the Bank has agreed
that the Borrower’s national Procurement Arrangements may be applied, the Borrower should
consider how price adjustment mechanisms will be addressed in the most appropriate manner.

Price adjustment provisions are usually not necessary in simple contracts involving delivery of Goods or
completion of Works, Non-consulting Services and Consulting Services within 18 months, but they must
be included in contracts that extend beyond 18 months. Contracts of shorter duration can also include
similar provisions for price adjustments when future local or foreign inflation is expected to be high.

The market research analysis report should provide all the information needed to establish cost
trends and drivers in the market, as well as an assessment of the business environment of the
Borrower. The Borrower should use this information to justify the selection of price adjustments.

Request for Proposals
A Request for Proposals (RFP) is a competitive method for the solicitation of Proposals. It should

Request for Bids
A Request for Bids (RFB) is a competitive method for the solicitation of Bids. It should be used
when, because of the nature of the Goods, Works, or Services to be provided, the Borrower is able
to specify detailed requirements to which Bidders respond in offering Bids.

Procurement under this method is conducted in a single-stage process. Qualifying criteria,
minimum requirements normally evaluated on a pass/fail basis, are used with RFB. Rated-type
Evaluation Criteria are normally not used with this method.

Request for Quotations
A Request for Quotations (RFQ) is a competitive method based on comparing price quotations
obtained from suppliers. This method may be appropriate for procuring limited quantities of readily
available off-the-shelf Goods or Non-consulting Services, standard specification commodities, or
simple civil Works of small value, when it is more efficient than more competitive methods.

Direct Selection
Proportional, fit-for-purpose, and VfM considerations may require a direct selection approach:
that is, approaching and negotiating with only one firm. This selection method may be appropriate
when there is only one suitable firm or there is justification to use a preferred firm.

Selection Arrangements
The selection arrangements are:

   ◾	 Competitive Dialogue

   ◾	 Public-Private Partnerships (PPP)

   ◾	 Commercial Practices

   ◾	 United Nation (UN) Agencies

   ◾	 e-Reverse Auctions

   ◾	 Imports

   ◾	 Commodities

   ◾	 Community-Driven Development

   ◾	 Force Accounts

   ◾	 Framework Agreements

   ◾	 Cross-Project Opportunities

Competitive Dialogue
Competitive Dialogue is an interactive multistage selection arrangement that allows for dynamic
engagement with Proposers. It may be used only for complex or innovative procurement.

Public-Private Partnerships
A Public-Private Partnership (PPP) is a long-term contract between a private party and a
government entity, to provide a public asset or service, in which the private party bears significant
risk and management responsibility, and remuneration is linked to performance.

Commercial Practices
Commercial Practices refers to the use of the well-established Procurement Arrangements used
by the private sector—normally entities that are not subject to the Borrower’s public procurement
law—for the procurement of Goods, Works, or Non-consulting Services. Commercial practices
may also be used for a program of imports undertaken by private sector entities. The Bank’s Core
Procurement Principles are the standard for determining the acceptability of Commercial Practices.

United Nation (UN) Agencies
UN Agencies can be directly selected in situations where their expertise or rapid mobilization
on the ground is critical, in particular, in circumstances of urgent need of assistance or capacity
constraints.
66       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




     e-Reverse Auctions
     An electronic reverse auction, or e-auction, is a scheduled online event in which prequalified or
     registered suppliers bid against each other on their price.

     Imports
     When the Investment Project Financing (IPF) provides financing for a program of imports, such as
     a program for importing eligible essential Goods by private or public sector entities, based on a list
     agreed upon between the Borrower and the Bank.

     Commodities
     Procurement of commodities refers to procuring items such as grain, animal feed, cooking oil,
     fertilizer, and metals. The procurement of commodities often involves multiple awards for partial
     quantities to assure security of supply and multiple purchases over a period of time to take
     advantage of favorable market conditions and to keep inventories low.

     Community-Driven Development
     Procurement Arrangements, specifications, and contract packaging may be suitably adapted, in
     a way that is acceptable to the Bank, when, in the interest of Project sustainability, or to achieve
     certain specific social objectives of the Project, it is desirable (in selected Project components) to:

        ◾	 Call for the participation of local communities and/or non-governmental organizations
           (NGOs) in civil Works and the delivery of Non-consulting Services

        ◾	 Increase the use of local know-how, Goods, or materials

        ◾	 Employ labor-intensive and other appropriate technologies

     Force Accounts
     Force Account—which refers to Works such as construction and installation of equipment, as well
     as Non-consulting Services carried out by a government department of the Borrower’s country
     using its own personnel and equipment—may be the only practical method of procurement under
     specific circumstances. A government-owned construction unit that is not managerially, legally, or
     financially autonomous is considered a Force Account unit. The use of Force Account requires that
     the Borrower apply the same rigorous quality checks and inspection as for contracts awarded to
     third parties.

     Framework Agreements
     A Framework Agreement (FA) is an agreement with one or more firms that establishes the terms
     and conditions that will govern any contract awarded during the term of the FA, a call-off contract.
     The terms and conditions will usually include the fee rate, charge rate, or pricing mechanism.
     FAs may be established for the anticipated procurement of Goods, Works, or Services, when
     required over a specified period of time. An FA does not commit either party to procure or
     supply. Once established, a FA provides a fast and efficient way to procure Goods, Works, or
                                                                      PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS          67




Non-consulting Services. A multi-supplier FA allows a Borrower to select from a number of firms,
helping to ensure that each procurement represents the best VfM.

Cross-Project Opportunities
This stage involves undertaking a review of opportunities for using contracts that currently exist and
how requirements across Projects may be combined to increase leverage through economies of scale
and optimized resource planning and scheduling. In addition, these opportunities serve to reduce the
time spent by the Borrower putting contracts in place for a Project. These opportunities include:

   ◾	 Buying Goods and services from existing FAs if already established

   ◾	 Making use of other agreements, for example, leveraging UN Office for Project Services
      (UNOPS)contracts to buy vehicles

   ◾	 Leverage buying power across projects, for example, if more than one Project is being undertaken
      in a country and there is common expenditure, then buying can be done on a combined basis.


Market Approaches
In addition to choosing the approved selection method and arrangement, a market approach must
also be identified.

The World Bank’s Procurement Principles determine that an open, competitive approach to procurement
is preferred, as it is the optimum solution to achieving the Core Procurement Principles. The PPSD can be
used to justify an alternative approach if merited and justified by the Project’s/contract’s circumstances.

Types of Competition
The various types of competition include:

   ◾	 Open

   ◾	 Limited

   ◾	 International

   ◾	 National

   ◾	 No Competition–Direct Selection

These competition types are described below, along with a description of when they may be
appropriate to be used. The main source of information to make the decision on the type of
competition will be the market analysis report.

Open Competition
An open competitive approach to market is the Bank’s preferred approach, as it provides all
eligible prospective Bidders or Proposers with timely and adequate advertisement of a Borrower’s
68       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




     requirements and an equal opportunity to bid or propose for the required Goods, Works, or Services.
     Any approach, other than open competition, shall be justified by the Borrower.

     Limited Competition
     A limited competitive approach to market is by invitation only, without advertisement. It may be an
     appropriate method of selection where there are only a limited number of firms, or there are other
     exceptional reasons that justify departure from open competitive procurement approaches.

     International Competition
     Approaching the international market through international competitive procurement is appropriate
     when the participation of foreign firms will increase competition and may assure the achievement
     of best VfM and fit-for-purpose results.

     Open international competitive procurement, for which international advertisement is required in
     accordance with the Procurement Regulations, is the preferred approach for complex, high-risk,
     and/or high-value contracts.

     National Competition
     Approaching the national market may be appropriate when the procurement is unlikely to attract
     foreign competition because of:

        ◾	 The size and conditions of the market

        ◾	 The value of the contract

        ◾	 Activities that are scattered geographically, spread over time, or are labor intensive

        ◾	 The Goods, Works, or Non-consulting Services are available locally at prices below the
           international market

     Approaching the national market may also be appropriate when the advantages of approaching
     the international market are clearly outweighed by the administrative or financial burden involved.
     If foreign firms wish to participate in open national competitive procurement, they are allowed to do
     so on the terms and conditions that apply to national firms. When approaching the national market,
     the country’s own procurement procedures may be used.

     No Competition–Direct Selection
     Direct selection may be appropriate under the following circumstances:

        1.	 Single-Source Selection (preferred supplier from several sources):

           a.	 An existing contract that was not originally financed by the Bank, for Goods, Works, or
               services—awarded in accordance with procedures acceptable to the Bank—may be
               extended for additional Goods, Works, and Non-consulting Services of a similar nature,
               if it is properly justified, if no advantage may be obtained by competition, and if the prices
               are reasonable;
                                                                     PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS         69




      b.	 There is a justifiable requirement to reengage a supplier that has previously completed
          a contract with the Borrower to perform a similar type of contract. The justification shall
          show satisfactory performance of the supplier in the previous contract, that no advantage
          may be obtained by competition, and that the prices are reasonable;

      c.	 The procurement is both very low value and low risk, as agreed in the Procurement Plan
          (PP); and/or

      d.	 The case is exceptional, for example, in response to natural disasters and Emergency
          Situations.

   2.	 Sole-Source Selection (only one source possible):

      a.	 Standardization of Goods that need to be compatible with existing Goods may justify
          additional purchases from the original supplier. For such purchases to be justified, the
          advantages and disadvantages of another brand or source of equipment should have
          been considered on grounds acceptable to the Bank;

      b.	 The required equipment is proprietary and obtainable from only one source;

      c.	 The procurement of certain Goods from a particular supplier is essential to achieve the
          required performance or functional guarantee of an equipment, industrial Plant, or facility; or

      d.	 When the Goods, Works, or Non-consulting Services provided in the Borrower’s country by a
          State -Owned Enterprise (SOE), University, or research center are of a unique and exceptional
          nature because of the absence of suitable private sector alternatives; as a consequence of
          the regulatory framework; or because its participation is critical to Project implementation.


Number of Envelopes/Stages
Single Stage: One Envelope
Single-stage procurement is most appropriate when the specifications and requirements are
sufficient to enable submissions of complete Bids or Proposals. Single-stage, one-envelope
procurement requires submission of both technical and financial Bids or Proposals in one envelope.

Single Stage: Two Envelopes
If appropriate, a two-envelope process may be used in single-stage procurement. The first
envelope contains the qualifications and technical part, and the second envelope contains the
financial, or price, component; the two envelopes are opened and evaluated sequentially.

Multistage
It may often be impractical to prepare complete technical specifications in advance for the
procurement of

   ◾	 Large, complex facilities for which a turnkey contract will be awarded for the design and
      build of a Plant
70       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




        ◾	 Works of a complex and special nature

        ◾	 Complex information and communication technology subject to rapid technological advances

     In the first stage, Proposals are invited on the basis of a conceptual design, performance, or
     functional specification, subject to the Borrower conducting confidential discovery and clarification
     meetings to learn about possible solutions. In the second stage, the RFB or RFP document may be
     amended to reflect the discoveries made in the confidential meetings, and issued to the qualified
     Bidders or Proposers, requesting them to submit final Proposals. As required in the RFB or RFP
     document, the second stage may be submitted in one envelope or two envelopes for the technical
     and financial parts, respectively, where the two envelopes are opened and evaluated sequentially.


     Best and Final Offer
     Best and final offer (BAFO) is an option under which the Borrower invites Bidders or Proposers
     that have submitted substantially responsive Bids or Proposals to submit their best and final offer.
     Such a process may be appropriate when the Procurement Process would benefit from Bidders
     or Proposers having a final opportunity to improve their Bids or Proposals, including by reducing
     prices, clarifying or modifying their Bid or Proposal, or providing additional information.


     Negotiations
     Negotiations can take place following Bid or Proposal evaluations and before the final contract
     award. Any negotiation shall be in accordance with the requirements of the RFB/RFP document.
     Negotiations may involve terms and conditions, price, and/or social, environmental, and innovative
     aspects, as long as they do not change the minimum requirements of the Bid or Proposal.


     Pre and Post-Qualification and Initial Selection
     Prequalification and post-qualification are methods used to ensure that suppliers with appropriate
     skills, experience, track record capacity, and capability are invited to bid or are awarded contracts.
     It is a critical activity to ensure successful selection of suppliers and ultimately getting Projects
     delivered.

     This can take three forms:

        1.	 Prequalification

        2.	 Initial Selection

        3.	 Post-qualification

     Prequalification and Initial Selection are processes used to Shortlist Applicants in the procurement
     of Goods, Works, and Non-consulting Services. These processes ensure that only those with
     appropriate and adequate capacity, capability, and resources are invited to submit Bids or Proposals.
     In undertaking a Prequalification or Initial Selection, the Borrower shall use the Bank’s Standard
     Prequalification document or Initial Selection document, respectively.
                                                                     PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS         71




Prequalification or Initial Selection is appropriate for large or complex contracts, or in other
circumstances, such as the need for custom-designed equipment; Plant; specialized services;
some complex information and technology; procurement under turnkey, design, and build; or
management contracting, in which the high costs of preparing detailed Bids or Proposals could
discourage competition.

The assessment of an Applicant’s qualifications shall not take into consideration the qualifications
of other firms, such as its subsidiaries, parent entities, affiliates, subcontractors—other than
specialized subcontractors if permitted in the Prequalification or Initial Selection document—or any
other firm different from the firm that submitted the Prequalification or Initial Selection Application.

When the time elapsed between the Borrower’s decision on the list of prequalified or initially
selected Applicants and the issuance of RFB or RFP documents is longer than 12 months, the
Bank may require that a new Prequalification or Initial Selection process is conducted through
readvertisement.


Prequalification
Prequalification is normally used with RFBs and is optional, depending on the nature and
complexity of the Goods, Works, or Non-consulting Services. In Prequalification, minimum
requirements are normally assessed on a pass or fail basis against criteria such as eligibility,
experience, technical capability, and financial resources. These consider objective and
measurable factors such as experience, satisfactory past performance, successful completion of
similar contracts over a given period, capability of construction and/or manufacturing facilities,
financial situation, and eligibility. All Applicants who substantially meet the minimum qualification
requirements are invited to submit a Bid.


Initial Selection
Initial Selection is normally used with RFPs and should be used for all Competitive Dialogue
processes. It enables the Borrower to invite only the highest-ranked Applicants to submit Proposals.
Initial Selection involves a two-step process. The first step is similar to the Prequalification process.
This establishes the long list of Applicants. Long-listed Applicants are then evaluated against Rated
Criteria. After ranking the combined Rated Criteria scores, the Borrower selects the highest-ranked
Applicants to submit Proposals.


Post-Qualification
If Bidders or Proposers have not been prequalified or initially selected, the Borrower should
specify appropriate qualification requirements in the RFB or RFP document to verify that a Bidder
or Proposer that would be recommended for contract award has the capability and resources
to effectively carry out the contract. The assessment of a firm’s qualifications shall not take into
consideration the qualifications of other firms, such as its subsidiaries, parent entities, affiliates,
subcontractors (other than specialized subcontractors if permitted in the RFB/RFP document), or
any other firm different from the firm that submitted the Bid or Proposal.

     Selecting the Right Combination
     When determining the right combination of the activities to design the approach to market, the decision
     on the type of selection method is primarily driven by the level of competition in the market, the number
     of capable suppliers available, the specificity of the requirements, and the inherent risks involved in
     delivery. This information should come from the:

        ◾ Market Analysis, including the value and complexity of the contract as determined by the
          supply position model

        ◾ Market Engagement Strategy

        ◾ Procurement Risk Analysis

     Applying this information to each contract and then to the chosen combination of selection methods
     and market approaches should help keep all of the contracts that make up the Project consistent.


     Evaluation Methods
     The PPSD informs the evaluation method and Evaluation Criteria to be applied. For more information,
     see Procurement Guidance: Evaluating Bids and Proposals. Evaluation Criteria need to be
     appropriate to the type, nature, market conditions, and complexity of what is being procured and
     must be clearly specified in detail in the RFB or RFP document.

     When Rated Criteria are to be used, both the Rated Criteria and any subcriteria, are to be
     prioritized, scored, and weighted according to their relative importance. The number of Rated
     Criteria should be kept to the minimum.

     The following requirements govern the use of Bid or Proposal Evaluation Criteria:

        ◾ The Evaluation Criteria shall be proportionate and appropriate to the type, nature, market
          conditions, complexity, risk, value, and objective of what is being procured.

        ◾ The RFB or RFP document shall detail the complete Evaluation Criteria, including Rated
          Criteria and any subcriteria as applicable, and the specific way they shall be applied,
          including their prioritization and weighting. Rated Criteria weightings apply as detailed in
          the Matrix (see Annex C).

        ◾ Only the Evaluation Criteria, and all the Evaluation Criteria, indicated in the RFB or RFP
          document shall be applied in the assessment of Bids or Proposals.

        ◾ Once the RFB or RFP documents have been issued, any change to the Evaluation Criteria
          shall be made only through addenda.

        ◾ The Evaluation Criteria shall be applied consistently to all Bids or Proposals submitted.

     Value for Money
     The Evaluation Criteria must be designed to enable the Borrower to achieve the best Value for
     Money (VfM).
                                                                       PPSD SECTIONS AND INSTRUCTIONS       73




The principle of VfM means the effective, efficient, and economic use of resources, which requires
the evaluation of relevant costs and benefits, along with an assessment of risks, and non-price
attributes and/or life-cycle costs, as appropriate. Price alone may not necessarily represent VfM.

To achieve VfM, the Evaluation Criteria, including Rated Criteria where applicable, may include the
following factors:

        ◾	 Cost: Evaluation of cost using a methodology that is appropriate to the nature of the
           procurement, including

           ▪	 Adjusted Bid price

           ▪	 Adjusted Bid price plus the running/recurrent cost over the useful lifetime of the asset on
              net present cost basis (life-cycle costs)

        ◾	 Quality: Evaluation of quality using a methodology to determine the degree to which
           the Goods, Works, Non-consulting Services, or Consulting Services meet or exceed the
           requirements

        ◾	 Risk: Adequacy of the Bidder’s/Proposer’s methodology to identify and mitigate
           relevant risks, such as those related to environment, social, economic, cybersecurity, or
           implementation

        ◾	 Sustainability: The Bidder or Proposer’s approach and/or methodology that manages,
           protects, or enhances the overall sustainability risks and issues. These include the
           environmental and social aspects of the Project during planning and implementation.
           These may also include longer-term, qualitative and non-financial benefits.

        ◾	 Innovation: Innovative aspects of design, methodology, technology, and/or delivery of the
           Goods, Works, Non-consulting Services, or Consulting Services, among others.

Quality and Cost-based Selection
QCBS is a competitive process among Shortlisted consulting firms, under which the selection of
the successful firm takes into account the quality of the Proposal and the cost of the services.
The RFP document shall specify the minimum score for the technical Proposals. The relative
weight to be given to the quality and cost depends on the nature of the assignment. Among
the Proposals that are responsive to the requirements of the RFP document and are technically
qualified, the Proposal with the highest combined quality and cost score is considered the Most
Advantageous Proposal.


Fixed Budget-based Selection
Like QCBS, FBS is a competitive process among Shortlisted consulting firms, under which the
selection of the successful firm considers the quality of the Proposal and the cost of the services.
In the RFP document, the cost of services is specified as a fixed budget that shall not be exceeded.
FBS is appropriate when

   ◾	 The type of Consulting Service required is simple and can be precisely defined;

   ◾	 The budget is reasonably estimated and set; and

   ◾	 The budget is sufficient for the firm to perform the assignment.

The RFP document specifies the budget and the minimum score for the technical Proposals. The
Proposal with the highest technical score that meets the fixed budget requirement is considered
the Most Advantageous Proposal.


Least Cost-based Selection
Similar to QCBS, LCS is a competitive process among Shortlisted consulting firms, under which
the selection of the successful firm considers the quality of the Proposal and the cost of the
services. LCS is generally appropriate for assignments of a standard or routine nature, such
as engineering designs of non-complex Works, for which well-established practices and
standards exist.

The RFP document specifies the minimum score for the technical Proposals. Among the Proposals
that score higher than the minimum technical score, the Proposal with the lowest-evaluated cost is
considered the Most Advantageous Proposal.


Quality-based Selection
Under QBS, the Proposal quality is evaluated without using cost as an Evaluation Criteria. If the
RFP requests both technical and financial Proposals, the financial Proposal of only the highest
technically qualified firm is opened and evaluated to determine the Most Advantageous Proposal.
However, if the RFP document requests only technical Proposals, the firm with the highest-ranked
technical Proposal is invited to submit its financial Proposals for negotiations.

     Contract Management
     Contract management is a formal process that focuses on ensuring both the supplier and the
     Borrower meet their contractual commitments to time, cost, and quality. It requires systematic and
     efficient planning, execution, monitoring, and evaluation to optimize performance while managing
     risks to ensure that both parties fulfill their obligations with the ultimate goal of achieving VfM and
     contractual results. It involves

        ◾	 Tracking and monitoring of delivery and costs

        ◾	 Contract administration

        ◾	 Being clear on roles and responsibilities of both Borrower and Supplier

        ◾	 Managing relationships with both the supplier and key stakeholders

        ◾	 Being proactive throughout the contract to anticipate problems and issues before
           they arise

        ◾	 Managing problems and issues as they arise—quickly, effectively, fairly, and in a transparent
           manner.

     From the Borrower’s perspective, contract management

        ◾	 Ensures that the supplier delivers upon their commitments

        ◾	 Obtains best VfM during the life of the contract

        ◾	 Manages supply risks for the duration of the contract

        ◾	 Continually challenges to drive best value in its contracts

        ◾	 Ensures effective contracts that continue to deliver the PDOs

        ◾	 Demonstrates best procurement practice in the management of contracts

        ◾	 Provides evidence to support any audits.


     Contract Management Approach
     The approach to defining the contract management requirements should consider

        ◾	 An understanding of the level of influence the Borrower may have over the supplier, which is
           informed by the supply preferencing model analysis;

        ◾	 An understanding of how much influence the Borrower needs over the supplier, which is
           informed by the supply position model and procurement risk analysis;

   ◾	 How the supplier will be managed through review meetings, progress reports, risk logs, and
      action plans;

   ◾	 The key milestones and when they should be reviewed;

   ◾	 The level of resources required to manage the contract effectively; and

   ◾	 How performance will be assessed against pre-agreed measures, including KPIs.

The approach to contract management should be proportionate to the value, risk, length,
type, and complexity of the contract and the type of market or suppliers who will be fulfilling
the contract.

Depending on the nature of the relationship with the supplier, the level of effort may change for
the Borrower.

For simple and low-value contracts, the Contract Management Plan should include, as a minimum,
the following.

   1.	 Key roles and responsibilities

     delivery is fully aligned with the desired outcome.

     mechanisms and payment decisions, they will need to be agreed upon and included as part of the

Tools and Techniques
There are a number of procurement tools and techniques available to support market analysis that
help to not only structure the research, but also to analyze the information to draw conclusions and
develop the procurement approach.

The key tools and techniques to support market analysis are

   ◾	 Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats (SWOT) Analysis;

   ◾	 Porter’s Five Forces Analysis;

   ◾	 Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental (PESTLE) Analysis;

   ◾	 Supplier Preferencing;

   ◾	 Supply Positioning and Supply Preferencing; and

   ◾	 Supply Chain Analysis.

As with the overall preparation of the Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD), the
application of these tools needs to be proportionate to the level of market research and information
required to develop a fit-for-purpose procurement approach. In most instances, each of the tools
will need to be utilized.


SWOT Analysis
SWOT is an analysis of the internal influences on a Borrower’s organization, over which the Borrower
has an element of control. Opportunities and Threats are an analysis of external influences on a
Borrower’s organization, over which the Borrower tends to have far less control.

When using a SWOT analysis (figure XXIV), the objective is to help Borrowers develop a full
awareness of all the factors, positive and negative, that may affect the delivery of the procurement
and project. This analysis will assist with the identification of the procurement approach that will
best deliver the fit-for-purpose procurement approach and the project requirements.

Strengths are the qualities that enable the Borrower to deliver a successful project. Strengths
can be either tangible or intangible. These are seen as core expertise, including staff capability,
experience, financial resources, effective processes, and reputation.

Porter’s Five Forces Dimensions
According to Porter, the nature of competition in any industry can be considered on the basis of the
following five forces:

   1.	 Bargaining power of suppliers (supplier power)

   2.	 Bargaining power of buyers (buyer power)

   3.	 Rivalry among current competitors (competitive rivalry)

   4.	 Threat of substitute products/services (product and technology development)

   5.	 Threat of new potential entrants (new market entrants)


Bargaining Power of Suppliers
Bargaining Power of Suppliers refers to the potential for suppliers to increase their prices. When
suppliers are in a position of strength, they can improve their profits by increasing their prices.
A supplier’s strength can be determined by assessing:

   ◾	 The availability of substitutes. A strong supplier’s Goods, services, and Works tend to
      be unique.

   ◾	 The cost of switching to another supplier’s product. A strong supplier exists where
      the buyer (Borrower) has to pay high costs to switch to an alternative supplier’s
      product.

   ◾	 The importance of Goods, service, and Works to the buyer (Borrower). A strong
      supplier exists where the buyer has a strong dependency or need for the
      supplier’s product.


Bargaining Power of Buyers
Bargaining power of buyers refers to a buyer’s ability to drive down the prices charged by the
suppliers in the market, or to increase supplier costs in the market by demanding better quality and
service. The buyer’s strength can be determined by assessing:

   ◾	 The size of their requirement. A buyer is in a strong position when the requirement is large,
      either to the market in general or especially in relation to an individual supplier’s size within
      that market.

   ◾	 The ease with which the buyer can procure from other suppliers. A buyer is in a strong
      position when there are many other suppliers providing the same or similar services, Goods,
      and Works in the market, at a lower or similar cost, and the buyer is not subject to any
      switching costs.

   ◾	 The number of other buyers in the market. A buyer is in a strong position when there are few
      other buyers in the market for an individual supplier’s Goods, services, and Works.
90       PROJECT PROCUREMENT STRATEGY FOR DEVELOPMENT: PPSD LONG FORM DETAILED USER GUIDANCE




     Rivalry Among Current Competitors
     Rivalry among current competitors refers to the competitive struggle for market share between
     suppliers in a market. Extreme rivalry among established suppliers poses a strong threat to
     profitability. The strength of rivalry in a marketplace can be determined by assessing the

        ◾	 Extent of barriers to exit and entry

        ◾	 Amount of fixed costs a supplier has to bear

        ◾	 Competitive structure of industry

        ◾	 Presence of global customers

        ◾	 Absence of switching costs

        ◾	 Growth rate of the market

        ◾	 Demand conditions.


     Threat of Substitute Products
     Threat of substitute products refers to the availability of other Goods, services, or Works that
     have the ability to satisfy customer needs as effectively. Substitutes create an upper limit on the
     price that suppliers can charge in a market. The smaller the number of close substitutes, the greater
     the opportunity for a supplier to increase their prices.

     The combined impact and interrelationship of Porter’s Five Forces differs from market to market.
     Jointly, they determine the competition, profitability, and behaviors of suppliers within a market
     because they shape the prices that can be charged, the costs that can be borne, and the
     investment required to compete in the industry.

     From a Borrower’s perspective, this tool will help determine where the balance of power lies in
     the market and what level of influence and impact the Borrower’s requirements will have on the
     market. The tool is used to determine the type of market and ultimately the type of competition that
     exists within that market.


     Risk of Entry by Potential Competitors
     Risk of entry by potential competitors refers to suppliers who are not currently competing in the
     marketplace but have the potential to do so if given a choice. Entry of new suppliers increases the
     market capacity, increases competition for market share, and lowers the current prices. The threat of
     entry by potential competitors can be determined by assessing the extent of barriers to entry, such as

        ◾	 Economies of scale available to suppliers currently in the market

        ◾	 Customer brand loyalty to existing supplier Goods, services, and Works

        ◾	 Government regulation
                                                        ANNEX A: MARKET ANALYSIS TOOLS AND TECHNIQUES              91




   ◾	 Customer switching costs

   ◾	 Cost advantage enjoyed by existing suppliers in the market

   ◾	 Ease of distribution

   ◾	 The value of set-up costs and capital investment.

     Supplier Preferencing
     Supplier Preferencing is a tool that identifies how a supplier views a Borrower as a customer and,
     depending on this view, how the supplier is likely to behave. Supplier preferencing categorizes
     these views into four segments, as detailed in figure XXVI.

     In high-level terms, this tool will determine how attractive the Borrower is as a customer to the
     supplier. This in turn will determine the level of willingness or reluctance the supplier will exhibit in
     wanting to bid for work and the corresponding level of effort the supplier will make in meeting the
     Borrower’s requirements. This tool helps determine the extent that the Borrower can influence a
     market, through its procurement approach, to achieve the Project Development Objectives (PDOs)
     and procurement objectives (see section 7 of this Guidance).

     The outcome of this analysis should allow a Borrower to establish the following factors and to design
     a procurement approach to maximize market interest and actual participation in the procurement.

        ◾	 What changes does the Borrower need to make to be more attractive to the market?

        ◾	 How does the Borrower motivate the market to bid and provide a good solution that meets
           the Borrower’s requirements?

Supplier preferencing considers two dimensions in assessing the attractiveness of a Borrower and
the business (contracts) it has to offer:

   ◾	 The value of the business to the supplier

   ◾	 The overall attractiveness of the business to the supplier.

The first dimension is the value of the Borrower’s business, in terms of potential contracts, to the
supplier as a proportion of the supplier’s turnover. Turnover information can be obtained from a
supplier’s annual accounts or from other sources, such as market analysis reports. Normally, the
higher the proportion, the more motivated the supplier will be to bid for the contracts.

The second dimension is how attractive the Borrower’s business is to a supplier. Normally, the
higher the attractiveness, the more motivated the supplier will be to bid for the contract.

Using these two dimensions, the analysis can be placed into a Supplier Preferencing matrix by simply
classifying attractiveness and relative business value as low or high, as shown in figure XXIII.

Again, the value of a contract does not in itself make a procurement or a Borrower attractive to
a potential supplier. Rather, the whole package of value in addition to Borrower behavior informs
how the supplier is likely to react. The ideal classification is that potential suppliers will see
Borrowers as good customers they already treat as “core” or that that they would like to grow
the relative value of the account through “development.” Unfortunately, some suppliers may
see the Borrower as a “nuisance” to be avoided due to high costs of doing business, or as an
entity that can be “exploited” over the short term. Truthfully understanding this dynamic, and
how it can be changed to get the right, motivated supplier is critical in ensuring a successful
procurement result.

     Borrower Business Attractiveness
     Attractiveness is determined using a number of criteria. These include payment record, ease of
     doing business with the Borrower, compatibility with the supplier’s business strategy, any cultural
     affinities that may exist between the supplier and the Borrower, levels of trust, the Borrower’s
     business development potential, and the perceived image to the supplier of being associated with
     the Borrower or a specific Project.

     It is equally important to consider what makes a Borrower both attractive and unattractive, and this
     gives a clear indication of how the Borrower can increase overall attractiveness. The following are
     criteria to be considered in determining attractiveness:

     Attractive Characteristics

        ◾	 Profitability

        ◾	 In-country security

        ◾	 Stable/predictable government

        ◾	 Good future business planning

        ◾	 Manageable risks

        ◾	 Opportunities for additional business

        ◾	 Effective communication and information sharing

        ◾	 Cultural fit and understanding

        ◾	 A good “reference” project

        ◾	 Stable and reliable payment

        ◾	 Low levels of bureaucracy

        ◾	 Easy to service from an account or contract management perspective

        ◾	 Support from the World Bank

        ◾	 A transparent mechanism for handling Complaints.

     Unattractive Characteristics

        ◾	 High levels of bureaucracy

        ◾	 Reputation for onerous, lengthy, or expensive Procurement Processes

        ◾	 Poor planning

        ◾	 Slow, erratic, or uncertain payment

        ◾	 Uncertain or unmanageable risks

   ◾	 Security concerns

   ◾	 Low profitability—demanding contracts with low margins

   ◾	 Onerous terms and conditions

   ◾	 Turnover of personnel, leading to dysfunctional behavior

   ◾	 Does not do what they say they will do

   ◾	 Poor supply market capability

   ◾	 Immature supply chains

   ◾	 Logistically difficult to service

   ◾	 Suppliers unable to deliver effectively —through lack of information sharing and/or

   ◾	 Low levels of support from the World Bank.

It is not only a supplier’s capability and capacity to deliver the Borrower’s requirements that
delivers successful Projects, but also the supplier’s motivation and willingness to deliver what is
needed to meet the requirements. This is often harder to determine, but through the use of market
engagement and carefully worded questions in qualification and/or Bid and Proposal documents,
it is possible to validate or challenge the findings of supplier preferencing. Equally important is that
supplier preferencing should help the Borrower implement a plan of action to improve its general
attractiveness to market and operationalize this plan throughout the procurement approach
through careful design and preparation.

By applying the Supplier Preferencing model and determining which quadrant is applicable to a
Borrower, it is possible to identify the typical behaviors a supplier may exhibit when doing business
with that Borrower.

This classification is particularly helpful in identifying risk, as well as which suppliers within a market
should and should not be targeted. As mentioned, these conclusions need to be covered in the
market engagement plan, qualification exercise, supplier selection, Bid evaluation strategy, and risk
management plan.

Identifying the value to the Borrower of working with suppliers who behave in this manner is critical
in successful contracts and Projects. Therefore, these factors should be considered at both the
supplier qualification and Bid evaluation stages.

Bid evaluation using Rated Criteria offers the best opportunity for Borrowers to specify and assess
these attributes, values, and benefits across all Bids and Proposals. It will, of course, be important
to undertake the necessary due diligence to ensure that the supplier will actually operate in this
manner, because suppliers often bid exhibiting the Core qualities, but later revert to behaviors
considered Nuisance or Exploitable during delivery.

Nuisance
Borrowers that are considered Nuisance will be given minimum attention, and the supplier is likely
to seek to withdraw from any current contracts and to not pursue any new bidding opportunities.
Suppliers will rank a Borrower as very low on the list of priorities, as the view of the Borrower will
be typically characterized as

   ◾	 Little profit to be made;

   ◾	 Difficult and/or expensive to service; and/or

   ◾	 Poor at paying bills.

As a result of a Borrower being considered Nuisance, the supplier is likely to offer poor service and
show very little interest in resolving any delivery, quality, or contractual issues. Correspondingly,
Borrowers should be very cautious about receiving Bids from such a supplier and should also
consider whether they should try to exit any existing contracts.

Exploitable
Borrowers that are considered Exploitable will be ranked highly by suppliers due to the value of
business, but the supplier will seek short-term advantage from what the supplier will perceive to be
a position of strength.

As a result of the Borrower being viewed as unattractive, suppliers will seek to maintain the
business as long as it does not involve any particular effort for them. The opposite may also occur,
in which effort is applied by the supplier; the supplier seeks to exploit the Borrower by raising
prices on existing contracts or submitting Bids with higher prices, which, if they win, will continue
the pattern of increasing prices.

Suppliers may also seek to reduce their own costs through minimizing the costs of serving the
Borrower. Price increases can occur beyond straightforward price increases. Borrowers can
anticipate requests for contract variations for volume increases, time extensions, and unforeseen
circumstances.

Working with these suppliers will be a challenge for the Borrower. As a result, the Borrower’s
contract management may need to be strengthened to ensure on time-delivery, quality, and the
required specifications within budget. However, the situation can be recovered if the Borrower is

      able to identify why the supplier sees them as unattractive and then seeks to address these issues.
      Issues can often be traced to low profitability and late payment.

      Suppliers will be prepared to risk losing the Borrower’s business. Suppliers and contracts will need
      to be carefully managed, and contingency plans may be needed to deal with the risk of a supplier
      withdrawing from contracts.

Supply Chain and Value Chain Analysis
A supply chain comprises the tiered network of suppliers involved in producing, handling, or
distributing Goods, services, and Works from raw materials through final delivery and usage
and disposal. It maps the flow and movement of Goods and services from their source to final
destination. A Borrower will buy Goods and services from many suppliers, each of which comprises
a supply chain in its own right. The upstream supply chains extend from the Borrower to the
suppliers and their supply chains back to the raw material, while the downstream supply chain
extends from the Borrower to the final destination, usage, and disposal of the Goods, services,
and Works. Figure XXIX illustrates a simple supply chain map.

Mapping the supply chain is a useful tool for identifying potential risks to the Borrower that may
occur upstream or downstream from the supplier. Typically, the longer the supply chain, the greater
the risk to delivery, as the supplier will have less control in managing each stage or supplier within
the overall supply chain. In other words, each step in the supply chain creates a dependency that is
not in the direct control of either the Borrower or the contracted supplier.

Many sources of information can help the Borrower map supply chains. The starting point is
desk-based research through the use of market analysis reports, searches on the internet,
and specialist industry magazines. Other sources include previous supplier Bid or Proposal
submissions.

      Borrowers and the Bank also have technical specialists who have a thorough understanding of
      both markets and suppliers within them.

      Finally, the most effective way of mapping supply chains is to go out and undertake market
      engagement by asking suppliers or industry representatives questions about how supply chains
      operate in a market or a region. Using this information, the Borrower should be able to identify
      each step and dependency in a supply chain and understand whether there are any inherent risks
      in how the supply chain operates.

      Undertaking this type of risk assessment at each stage of a supply chain will allow Borrowers
      to identify individual mitigation measures that need to be considered when designing the
      procurement approach. For example, these measures can range from not allowing subcontractors
      to be used by the supplier or allocating multiple contracts to several different suppliers to manage
      risks associated with shortages of supply.

      The second purpose of supply chain mapping is termed value chain analysis, which assesses
      whether each step in the supply chain adds value or could be considered unnecessary by
      not adding value, or worse, by creating greater risk. In both of these instances, this leads to
      unnecessary cost, thereby creating inefficiency. The procurement approach should be designed to
      consider how these inefficient activities could be reduced or eliminated.

      For example, typical procurement approaches focus primarily on contracting with the supplier at
      the end of the supply chain. Supply chain analysis encourages consideration of whether there is a
      more appropriate procurement approach that may involve contracting with multiple suppliers in the
      supply chain, especially if the analysis identifies stages that add no value and/or create increased
      risk. This type of procurement approach may allow complete stages in the supply chain to be
      removed.

      This combined supply chain and value chain analysis is another tool that focuses the procurement
      approach on reducing threats in terms of risks and taking advantages of opportunities through
      reduction or elimination of activities that do not add value.

Specification Writing
When drafting and assuring the quality of specifications, the Borrower should:

   ◾	 Address the risks identified in the procurement risk assessment

   ◾	 Identify key deliverables based on the Project Development Objectives (PDOs)

   ◾	 Clearly state the requirements based on the PDOs

   ◾	 Consider future needs, especially if the contract, Project delivery, or asset operation is
      spread over a number of years

   ◾	 Use plain language

   ◾	 Be technically accurate

   ◾	 Be easy to read

   ◾	 Contain clear timescales

   ◾	 Set Specific, Measurable, Attributable, Relevant, and Time-Bound (SMART) performance
      criteria

   ◾	 Use appropriate standards where they exist

   ◾	 Reflect whole-life costs, if appropriate

   ◾	 Include health and safety considerations

   ◾	 Provide flexibility for subsequent requirements.

Equally, when drafting specifications, the Borrower should avoid:

   ◾	 Breaching copyright or propriety rights

   ◾	 Excluding areas of risk that are best addressed in the specification

   ◾	 Using trade names

   ◾	 Using brand names

         ◾	 Excluding site information where it is critical that the supplier is aware of health and safety
            implications, access arrangements, or security arrangements

         ◾	 Using needless acronyms or technical language

         ◾	 Discriminating on the basis of nation, state, or region

         ◾	 Using ambiguous language

         ◾	 Being biased toward any particular supplier, brand, or country.

      It should be noted that the type of specification used will determine the ultimate selection of the
      Standard Procurement Documents (SPDs) for Goods, Works, and services, with the exception of
      Consulting Services.

      Conformance specifications are used with a Request for Bids (RFB), while performance specifications
      are appropriate for a Request for Proposals (RFP), which includes both Competitive Dialogue and
      Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs).

      Conformance specifications are normally evaluated on the basis of lowest conforming price, while
      performance specifications are normally evaluated using Rated Criteria.



      Additional Sustainable Procurement Requirements
      Additional Sustainability requirements beyond those required by the Bank may arise from one or
      more of the following sources:

         ◾	 Borrowers’ policies on economic, social, and environmental sustainability, such as equality
            requirements, health and safety requirements, vehicle emission standards, or packaging

         ◾	 Risks and opportunities identified through analysis of the market or the business and
            operating environment. Sustainability risks and opportunities that are to be managed
            through the Procurement Process may also be identified through the Environmental
            and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA). The Bank requirements for ESIA are outlined in
            Environmental and Social Standards 1 (ESS1).

      Where possible, the additional sustainable procurement requirements should be based on evidence
      in the form of supporting data, existing social-label criteria, eco-label criteria, or on information
      collected from stakeholders in industry, civil society, and international development agencies.

      The Borrower may adopt international sustainability standards covering a wide range of product
      and service groups, provided they are consistent with the Bank’s Core Procurement Principles.

      Specifications should address additional specific sustainability requirements through either
      precise conformance (technical) or performance specifications. These requirements should set a
      sustainability standard that is to be met by suppliers or that allows suppliers to propose their ideas,
      innovations, and approaches to managing the sustainability risk.

Additional sustainable procurement requirements may specify materials to be used in production
and/or the method of production, packaging, or service delivery. However, all conformance

Rated Criteria for the Assessment of Quality
Rated Criteria are non-price factors that are considered when evaluating Bids and Proposals, which
enable the evaluation of quality aspects of each Bid or Proposal. Examples include:

   ◾	 Quality of methodology and work plan

   ◾	 Performance, capacity, functionality

   ◾	 Key personnel

   ◾	 Environmental and social risks and impacts

   ◾	 Sustainability

   ◾	 Resilience-building

   ◾	 Social value

   ◾	 Supply chain assurance

   ◾	 Cybersecurity.

For more information on Rated Criteria, refer to Annex C.



Evaluating Sustainable Procurement
Evaluation Criteria may require additional sustainable procurement technical competence to be
demonstrated beyond the Bank’s requirements. In practical terms, this may mean that the Bidder:

   ◾	 Has previous experience in successfully executing contracts with similar requirements;

   ◾	 Employs, or has access to, personnel with the required educational and professional
      qualifications, practices, and experience to deal with the sustainable procurement elements
      of the contract;

   ◾	 Owns, or has access to, the necessary technical equipment for environmental protection; and/or

   ◾	 Has the means to deliver on the sustainable procurement aspects of the contract.

Rated Criteria
Rated Criteria are non-price factors that are considered when evaluating Bids and Proposals. These
factors measure quality aspects. Examples of non-price factors include:

   ◾	 Quality of methodology and work plan

   ◾	 Performance, capacity, functionality

   ◾	 Key personnel

   ◾	 Environmental and social risks and impacts

   ◾	 Sustainability

   ◾	 Resilience-building

   ◾	 Social value

   ◾	 Supply chain assurance

   ◾	 Cybersecurity.


The Value of Rated Criteria
Rated Criteria are designed to address the specific risks, opportunities, issues, and quality factors
in each procurement. This flexibility supports a fit-for-purpose approach to the evaluation of
Proposals. Contracts are awarded based on the optimal balance of quality and cost, which is not
necessarily the lowest price. This represents the best Value for Money (VfM).

As part of the evaluation approach, when identifying Rated Criteria, Borrowers should ensure such
criteria focus on the most essential qualitative technical aspects of the procurement so that they
achieve the appropriate impact in the final award decision. To be transparent, the relevant details of
the evaluation approach and the detailed explanation of the Evaluation Criteria and Rated Criteria,
which cover technical aspects, including any Subcriteria, as well as the method to evaluate financial
cost, in addition to all of the weightings that will be applied, must be specified in detail in the
Procurement Documents.

      Prioritization
      Rated Criteria are prioritized and weighted according to their risk-level, value and relative
      importance to the Borrower in meeting its requirements. It may be useful at this stage to refer to the
      procurement-related risks identified in the Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD)
      and/or the Procurement Plan.

      One way of managing or mitigating procurement-related risks is to ask Bidders or Proposers in the
      Request for Bids (RFB) or Request for Proposals (RFP) how they will mitigate those risks and
      whether they anticipate other applicable risks and mitigations not yet identified by the Borrower.
      Rated Criteria can then be included in the RFB or RFP to allocate points to the Bid or Proposal,
      depending on how well the Bidder or Proposer has proposed to mitigate the risk, with additional
      points for identifying other anticipated relevant risks and effective mitigations.

      Generally, the overall number of Rated Criteria should be kept to the essential minimum. Having
      too many Rated Criteria often serves to dilute the important characteristics of Bids and Proposals
      and makes identification of the optimal Bidder or Proposer more difficult.

      To enable evaluation of overall technical factors—for example, quality, sustainability, environmental
      and social, among others—specific technical Subcriteria with corresponding weights may also be
      included if appropriate. It is important to ensure that the level of technical weighting is appropriate,
      both for the general technical aspects and Subcriteria as applicable, and then for the final evaluation,
      in which total technical scores are combined with financial cost scores by applying an overall
      weighting to financial cost versus technical aspects. The Rated Criteria weightings must be applied as
      detailed in the Matrix (see Annex C).

      Using Rated Criteria more widely will increase Borrowers’ flexibility to procure Goods, Works, and
      Non-consulting Services best suited to their specific situation and adopt a more fit-for-purpose
      approach. Rated Criteria will also increase the willingness of the best Bidders and Proposers to
      Bid and participate in Bank-financed Procurements, with the knowledge that their added value will
      be duly considered as part of the evaluation process, thereby increasing different supply options,
      global competition, and effective market solutions to solve the Borrower’s procurement challenges.
      Increasing the use of Rated Criteria further contributes to successful contract outcomes and effective
      risk management, including managing issues such as sustainability; environmental, social, and supply
      chain disruption; cybersecurity; global health emergencies; and others. Rated Criteria also contribute
      to combating Bid rigging and reducing the risk of collusion in the Procurement Processes. Combined
      with financial cost and, where appropriate, life cycle cost formulas, Rated Criteria provide a truer
      assessment of value that focuses on quality, sustainability, and other key criteria.


      Expanding the Use of Rated Criteria
      Since 2016, the World Bank’s Procurement Framework has enabled Borrowers to use Rated
      Criteria to evaluate nonprice factors such as technical matters, quality, sustainability,
      environmental, social, innovative aspects of Bids, and so on, when determining an award decision.

      A key objective of the Procurement Framework is to enable Borrowers to adopt a more strategic
      approach to procurement, including the use of Rated Criteria that prioritizes fit-for-purpose
      solutions rather than only the lowest-evaluated price.
